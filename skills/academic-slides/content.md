# Academic Content Guidelines

What goes into an academic talk and in what order. Layouts, design and Google Slides compatibility are in SKILL.md.

## 1. Argument

**One argument per talk.** A talk is not the paper read aloud. Pick the claim the time slot can carry; the rest goes to the appendix or stays in the paper.

**Pick a narrative spine** and keep to it:

| Spine | Order | Suits |
|-------|-------|-------|
| Situation, complication, resolution | What is known → what is missing or wrong → what this work contributes | Most conference talks |
| Funnel | Broad context → specific gap → approach → findings → implications | Seminars, defenses |
| Answer first | Conclusion → evidence for it | Grant panels, short slots, senior audiences |

**State the research question early,** on its own slide, by slide 3. The audience judges everything after it against the question.

**Each slide has one job in the argument.** If a slide could move anywhere in the deck without loss, it is misplaced or unneeded. If it does two jobs, split it.

## 2. Deck architecture

In order:

1. **Title:** the talk's claim or question as the title, authors and affiliations, venue, date.
2. **Motivation (1–2 slides):** why the problem matters and the gap in prior work.
3. **Research question (1 slide):** stated precisely, on its own slide.
4. **Methods (1–2 slides):** only what the audience needs to judge the results. For quantitative work: design, data, key variables. For qualitative work: sources, analytical approach. Details go to the appendix.
5. **Results (one finding per slide):** the title states the finding; the slide holds one exhibit.
6. **Discussion (1–2 slides):** what the results mean for the question, and the main limitation, stated before the audience raises it.
7. **Conclusions (1 slide):** two to four takeaways and contact details (email, link to the paper or preprint). It stays on screen through Q&A, so nothing follows it in the talk.
8. **References:** every source cited in the deck.
9. **Appendix:** labeled slides ("Appendix A: Robustness checks") for robustness checks, extra results, derivations, and answers to likely questions.

Decks over 15 slides get a section divider before each major part.

## 3. Exhibits

**One exhibit per results slide:** a chart, table, diagram, equation block or image. Two charts making one comparison become one chart; two points become two slides.

**The exhibit must prove the title.** Cover the exhibit: does the title still need it? Cover the title: is the takeaway visible in the exhibit? If not, sharpen the title or mark the finding.

**Mark the key finding** so nobody has to search: the focal series in the accent color with the rest gray, a shaded region, or one short callout phrase with a thin leader line, never a box.

**Graphs over tables for results.** Use a table only when exact values matter; full tables go to the appendix.

**Don't paste figures from the PDF.** Paper figures have print-size fonts and captions that don't read on a screen. Use figures made at slide size.

**Only show what you will discuss.** An exhibit you won't talk about goes to the appendix.

## 4. Citations

- Every claim, number or figure that isn't the speaker's own work gets a footnote on its slide, as a superscript number after the claim and an 11 pt muted footnote at the bottom of the slide, with the author-year reference in the footnote: "¹ Heckman et al. (2013), *Science*."
- A borrowed figure gets "Source: Jones et al. (2021), Fig. 3" under it. Reusing a published figure may need the publisher's permission; mention it to the user.
- Use one citation style through the deck, in the field's convention.
- The References slide lists the full reference for every footnote, in the same style.
- Cite only sources the user gave or you read; never invent one.

## 5. Timing

- Plan about 1.5–2 minutes per content slide. A 15-minute talk holds about 8–10 content slides, plus the title, references and appendix.
- Aim to finish 1–2 minutes early; conference sessions run to a strict schedule.
- Tell the user which slides can be skipped if time runs short. Methods and motivation compress first; the research question, key result and conclusions never do.

## 6. Audience

- **Q&A:** list the three to five likeliest questions ("Why not method X?", "Is the sample representative?") and prepare an appendix slide for each.
- **International audiences:** define every acronym on first use, avoid idioms, keep sentences plain.
- **Accessibility:** strong contrast between text and background; never color alone to carry meaning (add labels or shapes); alt text on figures if the deck will be shared.
